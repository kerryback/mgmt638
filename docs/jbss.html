Every number on the Baseline tab is a suggestion, not a conclusion. They are a defensible starting point drawn from the history and from what management has said, and they are where the argument begins rather than where it ends. Each driver gets a section of its own below: what its history shows, what actually makes it move, what would make you choose differently, and the quoted evidence. The decision is yours.

Driver by driver

Sales growth

History 2022 11.3%, 2023 4.6%, 2024 6.7%, 2025 3.8%, 2026 6.2%  ·  Suggested 4.5%, 6.5%, 6.0%, 4.5%, 3.5%, then 3.5% forever  ·  Moving it 1pp each way moves the value 38.2%

Two things drive this line and they move independently: pounds and price per pound. FY2026 is the case in point -- sales rose 6.2 percent while volume fell 2.5 percent, so the growth was price recovering nut costs, not more product out the door. Decide what you believe about each. For volume, the question is how fast the new high-speed bar lines fill; management sized the prize at over $300 million and, pressed on timing, guessed three to five years, which is a guess and was offered as one. Five years of that on a $1.18 billion base is roughly 5 percent a year of incremental growth; three years is far more front-loaded. For price, ask whether nut cost inflation keeps passing through -- it has been, but it cuts both ways when costs fall. The terminal rate is a different question entirely: it is a nominal perpetual rate for a mature packaged-food business, so anything much above nominal GDP has JBSS eventually becoming the economy.

“We are very optimistic about securing new distribution in the near future. And we estimate over $300 million in potential new growth for JBSS as we sell the capacity on these lines.”Jeffrey T. Sanfilippo, CEO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt
“I thought I had to guess somewhere between 3 to 5 years.”Jeffrey T. Sanfilippo, CEO, asked how long the $300 million would take, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt
“significant external uncertainties remain including tariffs, inflation, unpredictable commodity costs, and broader macroeconomic challenges”Jeffrey T. Sanfilippo, CEO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt

Why the suggestion sits where it does. Takes the $300 million at the slow end of the three-to-five-year range, on top of low base growth: FY2027 near trend because the lines only reach full operation in the second quarter, the ramp in FY2028 and FY2029, then a fade. Adds about $324 million of sales over the five years.

EBITDA margin

History 2021 12.1%, 2022 11.1%, 2023 11.1%, 2024 10.3%, 2025 10.1%, 2026 10.0%  ·  Suggested 10.2%, 10.6%, 10.8%, 10.8%, 10.8%, then 10.8% forever  ·  Moving it 1pp each way moves the value 29.8%

The margin has fallen every year since FY2021, 12.1 to 10.0 percent, so the first decision is whether that is a trend or a run of bad years. Separate the drags that should not repeat from the ones that should. FY2026 carried $2.7 million of costs from a third-party dry milk powder recall and manufacturing inefficiencies from onboarding a large contract manufacturing customer; both are transitory on their face. Against that, raw input cost per pound rose 12.1 percent, and mix is moving toward contract manufacturing and commercial ingredients, which carry lower margins than branded consumer -- that is structural, not a bad year. A margin that recovers all the way to 12 percent is implicitly a bet that mix reverses. One that stays at 10 percent is a bet that the new lines add no operating leverage at all, having just been built for it.

“Gross profit was also adversely affected by higher customer claims, higher snack bar ingredient costs, manufacturing inefficiencies, higher freight expense”Frank S. Pellegrino, CFO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt
“The weighted average cost per pound of raw nut and dried fruit input stock on hand increased 12.1% due to higher pecan and almond acquisition cost partially offset by lower walnut acquisition costs.”Frank S. Pellegrino, CFO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt
“Key areas of focus include AI enabled process enhancements, plant efficiency, SKU rationalization, trade spend effectiveness, procurement savings, and supply chain optimization.”Jeffrey T. Sanfilippo, CEO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt

Why the suggestion sits where it does. A partial recovery to 10.8 percent -- the recall and the onboarding inefficiencies lapse, the new lines add some leverage, but the mix shift and nut costs stop it short of the FY2021 level.

Next-year sales / net PP&E

History 2021 8.16×, 2022 8.54×, 2023 8.83×, 2024 7.32×, 2025 7.12×  ·  Suggested 5.25×, 5.60×, 5.90×, 6.15×, 6.30×, then 6.30× forever  ·  Moving it 5% of itself each way moves the value 5.5%

The assumption where the balance sheet is shouting -- though not, it turns out, the one the answer is most exposed to: the Drivers sheet puts a five percent move here at about a tenth of what a percentage point on sales growth or EBITDA margin does. What it governs is the shape of capital spending, which is worth getting right for its own sake. Net PP&E is stated as a turnover on NEXT year's sales, because a plant comes on line before the volume does. The turnover ran 8.2 to 8.8 through FY2023 and then fell to 7.1 -- and at FY2026 the balance sheet carries $86.9 million of construction in progress, 15.1 percent of gross PP&E against under 2 percent in every prior year. That is capacity in the ground producing nothing yet. So the honest starting point for year 1 is a turnover well below the historical range, and the real question is how far it recovers and how fast. If you believe the $300 million sells through, the turnover climbs back toward 7 or 8 as the same assets carry far more sales. If you believe the capacity was overbuilt, it stays near 5 and the valuation falls a long way. Note the mechanical consequence: capex is the plug here, so a rising turnover means net PP&E grows slower than sales and capex stays near depreciation. Check the implied capex line in the forecast against the $20 to $50 million the company spent before this build, and against the $88 million it spent in FY2026.

“Our second priority is to expand our bar portfolio and sell through the significant new manufacturing capacity we have added at our Elgin facility. Our engineering team has done an outstanding job bringing the new high speed bar lines we purchased online and we expect them to be fully operational by the second quarter of fiscal 2027.”Jeffrey T. Sanfilippo, CEO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt
“The 2 high speed lines are really balancing out our manufacturing capability... much like we did for snack nut and trail, we developed our manufacturing capabilities to run low volumes as well as high volumes very efficiently, and that is really what this investment represents.”Jasper Sanfilippo, COO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt

Why the suggestion sits where it does. Starts at 5.25, near where FY2026 sales-to-PP&E actually lands once the construction in progress is counted, and recovers to 6.30 rather than back to the old 8-plus, on the view that the capacity sells through but the asset base is permanently larger.

Depreciation / prior net PP&E

History 2022 15.6%, 2023 17.5%, 2024 20.3%, 2025 17.8%, 2026 17.1%  ·  Suggested 14.0%, 13.5%, 13.0%, 13.0%, 13.0%, then 13.0% forever  ·  Moving it 1pp each way moves the value 4.8%

Depreciation is a rate on PRIOR-year net PP&E, so the rate and the asset base move together and you should not read the history literally. It ran 15.6 to 20.3 percent, but on a base roughly half today's: accumulated depreciation is 58 percent of gross PP&E at FY2026, an old base being depreciated fast. The new high-speed lines are long-lived, so the blended rate should fall as they come in. A useful cross-check is the realised-capex line in the history, which backs capex out of the PP&E roll-forward: where it diverges from reported capex, acquired assets are usually the reason -- FY2024's gap is the $59 million Lakeville acquisition. Remember the rate only reaches the valuation through the tax shield and through capex, since capex is the plug; it is not worth agonising over to the decimal.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. Fades from 14 to 13 percent, below the 16-to-20 percent history, because the new long-lived lines dilute an old and heavily depreciated base. Implies roughly a seven-to-eight-year average life.

Accounts receivable

History 2021 7.7%, 2022 7.3%, 2023 7.3%, 2024 8.0%, 2025 6.9%, 2026 7.2%  ·  Suggested 7.2%, 7.1%, 7.1%, 7.0%, 7.0%, then 7.0% forever

Receivables have sat in a narrow 6.9 to 8.0 percent of sales band for six years, which is what you would expect from a business selling to large grocery, club and mass customers on standard terms. Absent a change in customer mix or terms there is little to argue about. The thing that would move it is the contract manufacturing growth, if those customers pay on different terms than the consumer channel.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. 6.9 to 8.0 percent of sales across FY2021 to FY2026, at 7.2 percent in FY2026. Held near there, easing toward the middle of the range.

Inventories

History 2021 17.2%, 2022 21.4%, 2023 17.3%, 2024 18.4%, 2025 23.0%, 2026 20.9%  ·  Suggested 20.9%, 20.5%, 20.0%, 19.7%, 19.5%, then 19.5% forever

The largest working-capital item and the most volatile, 17.2 to 23.0 percent of sales, because it holds nuts bought at harvest and carried through the year. Two forces to separate: pounds on hand, which follows volume, and cost per pound, which follows the crop. FY2026 sits near the top of the range at 20.9 percent with raw input cost per pound up 12.1 percent, so part of that is price and would unwind if nut costs fell. Consider forecasting this as days of cost of sales instead, which separates the two more cleanly than a percent of sales does.

“The decrease was driven by lower finished goods inventories for bars, lower walnut acquisition costs, and lower on hand quantities of pecans and walnuts.”Frank S. Pellegrino, CFO, transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt

Why the suggestion sits where it does. 17.2 to 23.0 percent of sales historically, at 20.9 percent in FY2026 -- the high end, because nut costs are high. Management reported the weighted average cost per pound of raw input stock up 12.1 percent (transcripts/JBSS_FY2026Q4_earnings_call_2026-08-20.txt). A slow drift down as bar finished goods normalise, not a return to the FY2021 level.

Prepaid expenses and other current assets

History 2021 1.0%, 2022 0.9%, 2023 0.7%, 2024 1.1%, 2025 1.3%, 2026 1.5%  ·  Suggested 1.5%, 1.4%, 1.3%, 1.2%, 1.2%, then 1.2% forever

Small and stable at 0.7 to 1.5 percent of sales. Hold it flat near the recent level unless something in the notes says otherwise; it is not worth a forecast of its own.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. 0.7 to 1.5 percent of sales historically, at the top of that range in FY2026; reverts toward the middle.

Accounts payable

History 2021 5.7%, 2022 5.0%, 2023 4.3%, 2024 5.0%, 2025 5.5%, 2026 6.2%  ·  Suggested 6.2%, 6.0%, 5.8%, 5.6%, 5.5%, then 5.5% forever

Payables are a source of cash, so a higher ratio raises the valuation -- which is a reason to be careful rather than generous. The range is 4.3 to 6.2 percent of sales and FY2026 is at the top of it. Ask whether that reflects genuinely longer terms or just the timing of nut purchases at year end; a June fiscal year end sits in an awkward place relative to the harvest. Check the notes for any supply-chain finance programme, which flatters payables in a way that reverses.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. 4.3 to 6.2 percent of sales historically, at 6.2 percent in FY2026. Reverts toward the mean.

Accrued payroll and related benefits

History 2021 2.8%, 2022 2.0%, 2023 2.8%, 2024 3.3%, 2025 1.7%, 2026 2.9%  ·  Suggested 2.9%, 2.8%, 2.7%, 2.6%, 2.6%, then 2.6% forever

The most volatile liability on the sheet, 1.7 to 3.3 percent of sales, because it carries incentive compensation that swings with the year's results. FY2025's 1.7 percent was a weak year and FY2026's 2.9 percent a better one. Forecast it toward the middle of the range rather than off either endpoint.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. 1.7 to 3.3 percent of sales and volatile with incentive accruals; reverts toward the mean.

Long-term workers' compensation liabilities

History 2022 0.8%, 2023 0.7%, 2024 0.7%, 2025 1.0%, 2026 0.9%  ·  Suggested 0.9%, 0.9%, 0.9%, 0.9%, 0.9%, then 0.9% forever

Self-insurance accrued on hours worked, so it scales with the workforce and therefore with volume rather than with price -- a reason to expect it to lag if growth comes from price. It has sat at 0.7 to 1.0 percent of sales throughout. The alternative classification is to call it a debt-like claim and put it in the bridge; that is defensible, and it would lower the equity value by roughly its book amount.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. 0.7 to 1.0 percent of sales, accrued on hours worked, so it scales with volume.

Cash tax rate on taxable income

History 2021 27.5%, 2022 26.0%, 2023 27.4%, 2024 27.8%, 2025 27.1%  ·  Suggested 27.0%, 27.0%, 27.0%, 27.0%, 27.0%, then 27.0% forever  ·  Moving it 1pp each way moves the value 3.5%

Cash taxes paid over pretax income ran 26.0 to 27.8 percent across FY2021 to FY2025, a tight band, and JBSS is an ordinary full taxpayer with no carryforward to model -- so this is the least contentious driver on the page. Use the marginal cash rate on taxable income, not an effective rate; the model derives the effective rate itself. The rate applies to EBIT, not to pretax income, because this is an unlevered valuation and the interest shield belongs in the discount rate. Changing it moves the answer roughly one for one with after-tax operating profit, so it is worth getting to the nearest point and not worth more than that.

Why the suggestion sits where it does. The middle of the 26-to-28 percent band the company has actually paid.

    Cost of capital

    Cost of equity on CAPM: a 4.2 percent risk-free rate, a 5.5 percent equity risk premium and a levered beta of 0.8 for a defensive packaged-food business gives 8.6 percent. Pretax cost of debt 5.5 percent, after tax at 27 percent gives 4.0 percent. Debt claims of $112.7 million against a materially larger equity value put the debt weight near 12 percent, for a WACC of about 8.0 percent. The beta and the debt weight are assumptions, not observations -- no market data was used, and this is the single number most worth arguing about.

    The model

    Sales grow at g. EBITDA is a margin on sales. Net PP&E at the end of year t is next year's sales divided by the sales-to-PP&E turnover — the one line that leads sales by a year, because capacity is built ahead of the volume it serves. Depreciation is a rate on prior-year net PP&E, and capex is the plug that reconciles the two; in steady state it lands at (g + d) times prior net PP&E. Every other operating asset and liability is a ratio to the same year's sales. Free cash flow is EBITDA less cash taxes on EBIT, less capex, less the change in working capital. Taxes are unlevered; the interest shield lives in the discount rate. A running NOL carryforward shelters the explicit years; anything still alive after year 6 is valued separately and appears in the bridge, so the perpetuity is taxed at the full rate and stays a genuine steady state.
