The Oldest Valuation Method There Is

A company is worth roughly what companies like it are worth.

No forecast, no discount rate, no terminal value. Find the peers, take the middle of their multiples, apply it. It is what a banker does on the back of an envelope, and it is a real benchmark — hard to beat and easy to check.

The whole method is three decisions: which multiple, which peers, and what to do with the ones that do not make sense.

Today We Need Three of Them

daily

pe, pb, ps, ev, evebit, evebitda, marketcap — one row per stock per day.

tickers

sector, industry, exchange, scalemarketcap. This is what makes a peer group a peer group.

sf1

The filings. Revenue, earnings, book value, and the ratios computed from them.

sf1 Has Dimensions

Annual Quarterly Trailing four quarters
As most recently restated MRY MRQ MRT
As originally reported ARY ARQ ART

Today we use MRY — annual, most recent restatement. It is the cleanest view of what a company earned. When we start backtesting, we will have to switch, because MRY tells you today what nobody knew at the time.

Three Things That Trip Everyone

Dates are text Cast before you compare or sort: date::DATE, reportperiod::DATE
sf1 has no date Use reportperiod for the fiscal period end, datekey for the filing date
Market cap is in thousands In daily. A $2T company shows up as 2,000,000,000

Each of these produces an answer, not an error. That is what makes them expensive.

Pulling It

Start by Looking Around

Tell GLM

Using the data portal, show me the columns in daily and tickers, and give me five rows of each.

Before any analysis, look at the actual rows. Half of the mistakes in this course get caught here, in thirty seconds, by someone who looked.

The Pull

Tell GLM

For the most recent date in daily, get ticker, pe, and marketcap for every stock. Join sector and industry from tickers. Then pull the MRY rows from sf1 for the most recent fiscal year and bring in revenue, net income, and shares outstanding. Show me the SQL before you run it.

That last sentence is the entire lesson from Tuesday, in six words.

Read the SQL

You are not checking whether it is elegant. You are checking four things.

Did it filter the dimension? Without dimension = 'MRY' you get six rows per company-year
Did it cast the dates? Text comparison on dates goes wrong quietly
Is the join key right? ticker is not unique across time — see actions
How many rows came back? You know roughly how many listed stocks there are

What a P/E of –12 Means

Nothing. It means the company lost money.

A negative P/E is not a cheap stock. It is a stock with no E. Left in the sample it drags the median in the wrong direction and makes the cheapest-looking companies the ones that are losing the most.

Tell GLM

Drop the negative P/E rows, and show me how many you dropped by sector.

Look Before You Compute

Tell GLM

Histogram of P/E across all stocks. Then the same by sector, and a table of the count, median, and the 10th and 90th percentiles in each.

You are looking for two things: how fat the right tail is, and whether the sectors actually differ. If they do not, the peer group is doing no work and the method has nothing to offer.

Median of Peers

Why the Median

The mean

One company with a P/E of 900 — a real company that barely broke even — and the average for the industry is meaningless.

The median

Unmoved by the tail. With ratios, where the numerator is bounded below and the denominator can approach zero, this is not a refinement. It is the difference between a number and noise.

Which Peers?

Sector

Eleven of them. Always enough companies, often not comparable ones.

Industry

Much finer. Sometimes four companies, and then the median is one company’s P/E.

Industry and size

Better in principle. Cuts the group again, so you need to watch the count.

There is no correct answer. There is a choice you make, write down, and defend when you present.

The Calculation

  1. Group the stocks by peer set, and drop the ones with no meaningful P/E
  2. Take the median P/E in each group
  3. Multiply it by the company’s own earnings per share
  4. Compare with the price it actually trades at

The gap is the whole output. A company trading well below its peer median is either cheap or different, and the method cannot tell you which.

Now Do It

Tell GLM

Compute the median P/E within each industry. For every stock, report its own P/E, its industry median, and the ratio of the two. Show me the twenty most expensive and the twenty cheapest by that ratio.

Read the two lists. If the cheap list is all one industry, or all tiny companies, something in the peer definition is doing work you did not intend.

Skills

Stop Re-Explaining Yourself

Always on

Applies to every conversation you have.

Who you are, how you want to be written to, what never to do.

Per project

Applies inside one folder.

This course, this dataset, its quirks.

Three scopes, and the third is the interesting one — because it costs nothing until it applies.

A Skill Is Written Instructions, Filed Where They Can Be Found

A folder with a SKILL.md in it. Two parts: a line saying when to use it, and the instructions for how.

At startup

It reads only the name and the one-line description — a few dozen words per skill.

When it matches

Only then does it open the file and read the rest.

Which is why you can have many

A hundred installed skills cost nothing until one of them is relevant.

Not a plugin, and not code. A written procedure.

CLAUDE.md Versus a Skill

CLAUDE.md — always read

What is always true here: what the project is, where things live, the conventions, what never to do.

Read at the start of every session in that folder.

A skill — read when relevant

A procedure for a particular kind of job.

Standing facts go in CLAUDE.md. Procedures go in skills.

Ask GLM to write the first CLAUDE.md for you — it will read the folder and draft it.

Why This Matters to a Group

The skill is a file. The file is in the repository.

Which means the decision is made once, by one of you, and the other two get it by pulling. It also means the decision is visible: when you present, the skill is the record of what you chose and why.

Every ad-hoc instruction typed into a prompt window dies with that conversation.

Build One

Get It Right Once, Then Save It

  1. Get the peer-median valuation working the way you want it, in a conversation
  2. Say: save that as a skill
  3. Start a fresh session and ask it to value a different company

Step 3 is the test. If it needs to be told anything you already decided, the skill is not finished.

What Has to Be Written Down

The decisions

Which multiple. Which peer definition. Median, not mean. What the minimum group size is.

The traps

dimension = 'MRY'. Cast the dates. Market cap is in thousands. Drop negative earnings.

The output

What it should hand back every time: the peer set and its size, the median, the implied value, the gap.

The middle card is the one that pays for itself. Those four traps will otherwise bite each of you, separately, for six weeks.

Exercise

  1. Build the skill in your group’s repository
  2. Test it on a company none of you used while building it
  3. Then test it on a bank, and on a company that lost money last year
  4. Commit and push it

Steps 2 and 3 are where you find out what you left implicit. Write down what broke — it is what you will present on Tuesday.

Where It Will Fail

The industry of one

A peer group with three companies in it has a median that is one company’s opinion.

Financials

P/E means something different for a bank. So does book value.

Growth

Two companies in the same industry with different growth rates should not trade at the same multiple. The method assumes they do.

The third one is what Thursday of next week is about: predicting the multiple instead of borrowing it.
