Stop Borrowing, Start Predicting

What the Peer Median Assumes

That every company in an industry deserves the same multiple.

Two software companies, same industry. One grows at twenty percent with a forty percent margin; the other grows at three percent and barely breaks even. The peer median says they should trade at the same P/E, and calls the difference mispricing.

It is not mispricing. It is the model being wrong.

The Alternative

Borrow a multiple

Find companies that look like this one. Use the middle of what they trade at.

Predict a multiple

Estimate what the market pays for growth, for profitability, for leverage. Apply those prices to this company.

The second one is a regression, and the first one is a special case of it — a regression on industry dummies and nothing else.

One Cross-Section

Freeze a Date

One row per company, on a single day. Three thousand rows, and no time dimension at all.

Tell GLM

Build a cross-section as of June 30 of last year: one row per stock, with P/E from daily, and from the most recent sf1 annual filing bring in ROE, revenue growth, the operating margin, leverage, and payout. Add sector.

Everything today happens inside this one table. The time series comes back at the end.

What Goes on the Left

P/E

Bounded below at zero, unbounded above, and violently skewed. OLS on this is mostly fitting the tail.

log P/E

Roughly symmetric. Coefficients read as percentage effects on the multiple, which is how you want to think about them anyway.

And the negative-earnings problem does not go away — you cannot take the log of a negative P/E, so those companies leave the sample. Note how many, and which kind.

What Goes on the Right

Growth Revenue growth, earnings growth — what the multiple is supposed to be paying for
Profitability ROE, ROA, margins
Risk Leverage, and how variable the earnings have been
Size Log market cap
Sector Dummies — which is where the peer median hides

Start with a handful you can defend. A regression with forty right-hand variables is a machine learning problem, and that is Tuesday.

Run It

Tell GLM

Regress log P/E on those characteristics with sector fixed effects. Winsorize the right-hand variables at 1 and 99 percent. Show me the coefficients, the standard errors, and the R-squared.

Then read the coefficients before you look at anything else. Does the market pay more for growth? For profitability? If a sign is backwards, that is the finding, and it usually means a variable is built wrong.

What R-Squared to Expect

Somewhere between a third and a half. Which is enormous, and does not mean what you want it to mean.

Cross-sectional valuation regressions fit well because companies genuinely do trade on their fundamentals. The question is not whether the fit is good. It is whether what is left over is worth trading.

Relative Value

Fitted and Residual

Fitted

What the market, on average, pays for a company with these characteristics.

Actual

What this company actually trades at.

Residual

The gap. Negative means it trades below what its own characteristics justify.

The residual is the signal, and it is exactly the same shape as last session’s signal — own multiple against a predicted one. All that changed is how the prediction is made.

Two Readings of a Negative Residual

It is cheap

The market has not noticed. Buy it, wait for the gap to close.

This is the assumption the whole strategy rests on.

Your model is missing something

The company is in litigation, or its growth is about to stop, and the market knows. The gap is information you failed to include.

You cannot tell these apart for any single company. You can only find out in aggregate, over many companies and many years, which is what the backtest is for.

Look at the Extremes

Tell GLM

Show me the twenty largest positive and twenty largest negative residuals, with their characteristics and their sector.

If the cheap list is all one sector, your sector dummies are not doing their job. If it is all microcaps, size is entering the pricing in a way your model has not captured. Either way you learn it in thirty seconds by looking.

Against Last Session

The peer median is the same regression with only sector dummies on the right.

Tell GLM

Fit it three ways on this cross-section: sector dummies only, characteristics only, and both. Compare the R-squared and the correlation between the three sets of residuals.

If the residuals correlate at 0.9, the extra machinery is not buying you a new signal. It is buying you the same signal with more steps.

Twenty-Five Cross-Sections

Refit Every Year

One regression per year, using only that year’s data.

The temptation is to fit once on everything and apply the coefficients throughout. That uses 2024 data to price a 2004 company. It is the same look-ahead as MRY, wearing a different hat, and it is harder to spot because nothing about the code looks wrong.

The Loop

  1. For each June 30 from 2001 to 2025, build the cross-section
  2. Fit the regression on that year alone
  3. Take the residuals
  4. Sort into deciles, long the cheapest, short the most expensive
  5. Hold twelve months, record the return

Step 2 is the only thing that changed from Tuesday. Everything else is the backtest you already have — which is the point of having written it as code you can read.

Watch the Coefficients Move

Tell GLM

Plot each coefficient by year, with its confidence band.

The price of growth is not a constant. If a coefficient flips sign in 2009 and again in 2021, that is worth a slide in your presentation — and it is a reason to be suspicious of any model that assumes the relationship is fixed.

How Many Companies Survive Each Year

Tell GLM

For each year, report how many stocks had all the variables and how many were dropped, broken out by which variable was missing.

Every characteristic you add shrinks the sample, and it does not shrink it randomly. A model that requires five years of earnings history is a model estimated on established companies only.

Did It Pay

Three Backtests, One Table

Peer median Tuesday’s result
Regression residual Today’s
Plain P/E No prediction at all — just buy the low multiples

Report the average annual return, the volatility, the t-stat, and the worst year for all three. Same dates, same universe, same weighting, or the comparison means nothing.

The Honest Outcome

It is entirely possible that plain P/E wins.

That would be a real result, and a well-known one. The elaborate model has more ways to be wrong, more parameters to fit to noise, and a smaller sample to fit them on. Report it if you find it.

What you must not do is keep trying specifications until one of them beats the others, and then present that one.
